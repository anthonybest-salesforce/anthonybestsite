# Putter Research: Newport 2 Replacement

**Research date:** May 2026  
**Objective:** Identify the best Scotty Cameron mallet replacement for the Newport 2, with honest consideration of competitor alternatives  
**Methodology:** Independent review of published equipment specs, on-course tracking data, peer-reviewed putting psychology research, and independent equipment reviews. No conclusion was fixed in advance; the recommendation follows from the evidence.

---

## 1. The Newport 2: What It Is and Who It Is Built For

The Scotty Cameron Newport 2 is one of the most successful putter designs in the history of golf. The design traces back to the early 1990s and is most associated with Tiger Woods, who won 14 of his 15 major championships using a Newport 2 GSS (German Stainless Steel) — his sole exception being the 1997 Masters, won with a Scotty Cameron Newport TeI3. All 15 of his major victories were with a Newport-style Cameron. The current production versions (Super Select Newport 2, Studio Style Newport 2) carry the same core architecture that made the original famous.

**Published specifications (current production):**

| Spec | Value |
|------|-------|
| Head material | 303 stainless steel |
| Face | Dual-milled (Super Select) / Studio Carbon Steel insert (Studio Style) |
| Neck | I-beam plumbing neck |
| Loft | 3.5° |
| Lie | 70° |
| Offset | Full shaft |
| Weighting | 2 × 40g (33"), 2 × 35g (34"), 2 × 30g (35") tungsten sole weights |

The Newport 2 is a blade putter. Its compact head, solid face construction, and low profile produce immediate, unfiltered feedback — you know exactly where you hit it on every putt. That is its greatest strength and its most significant limitation.

**Who it genuinely serves:**  
The Newport 2 performs best for players who putt frequently enough to keep their strike pattern consistently centered. This means players who practice putting regularly, play multiple times per week, or compete at a level where stroke repetition is constant. At that level, the Newport's feedback loop is an asset — it reinforces good habits in real time.

**What it costs everyone else:**  
When contact drifts even slightly off center, a solid-face blade with no perimeter weighting has no mechanism to compensate. The face twists. Distance and direction are both affected on the same putt. For a player who plays two or three times per week without dedicated putting practice between rounds, the off-center strike rate climbs — and the Newport penalizes each one directly.

This is not a design flaw. It is what the Newport 2 is engineered to do: deliver perfect feedback. The question is whether perfect feedback is what the player needs, or whether protection from occasional imperfection is the higher-value trade.

## 2. The Case for a Mallet: What the Data Says

### On-course tracking data

**Shot Scope, via MyGolfSpy (June 2025, 40,000+ on-course putts):**
- At **15-handicap**, mallet users made 82% of putts inside 6 feet; blade users made 75%
- At 15-handicap, mallet users averaged 2.3 three-putts per round; blade users averaged 2.6
- The 7-point make-rate gap represents roughly one additional make per round for a player with 8–10 makeable putts inside six feet

This is on-course data, not a putting mat study. The conditions are real greens, real pressure, real rounds. The gap is not theoretical.

**Methodological note:** The Shot Scope data does not control for selection bias. Players who choose mallets and players who choose blades may differ in ways that independently affect their putting — experience level, swing mechanics, teaching background, and how recently they fitted their equipment. The data shows a performance gap at 15-handicap but cannot definitively prove the gap is caused by putter shape alone. It is consistent with MOI providing a performance benefit, but correlation does not establish causation. Importantly, the same Shot Scope dataset shows the Scotty Cameron Newport 2 still appearing among the top-performing individual putter models at elite levels — demonstrating that the blade vs. mallet gap narrows significantly for skilled and consistent players.

**Golf Digest / Club Champion SAM PuttLab (2024):**  
Across all players tested, mallets outperformed blades 62% of the time. Face-to-path consistency was higher with mallets for every single player in the study — a +15% advantage in stroke reliability, across all skill levels.

**PGA Tour adoption (2024–2025):**  
Approximately 75–85% of the men's top 50 now use mallet putters. Mallets accounted for 75% of PGA Tour victories in 2024. Players with unlimited equipment access and dedicated fitting staff have made a decisive and near-uniform choice. This is not a trend. It reflects what the performance data has been showing for years.

### Why mallets outperform blades: MOI

MOI (Moment of Inertia) measures how much a putter head resists twisting when contact occurs off-center. A higher MOI number means less face rotation on mishits, which translates to less directional and distance loss.

A blade putter concentrates mass near the center of the head. A mallet distributes mass toward the heel and toe — as far from center as possible. The laws of rotational physics (I = Σ m·r²) mean that moving mass further from the center multiplies its contribution to twist resistance. Even a modest mallet delivers significantly more MOI than any blade of comparable weight.

Scotty Cameron does not publish MOI numbers for any of its putters. The relative differences between the Newport 2 and the Phantom models are real — the Phantom's wider, heavier perimeter bodies deliver substantially higher forgiveness than the Newport's compact blade geometry — but exact figures require independent lab measurement and vary by head weight and fitting.

### Motor learning and practice frequency

Research published in *Psychological Research* (2025) on cognitive and neurophysiological adaptations in golf putting motor learning confirms that putting precision is a perishable skill requiring thousands of consistent repetitions to remain fully grooved. Without regular practice between rounds, the neural pathways governing center contact are not reinforced, and strike pattern variability increases.

High-forgiveness equipment compensates for this variability automatically. A wider MOI window means that the putts lost in the first few holes of a round — while the player's touch is calibrating — are protected rather than penalized.

## 3. Confidence as a Measurable Performance Metric

This section covers research that is not widely cited in equipment marketing, but has direct implications for which putter is the right choice for a specific player.

### What the research shows

**Study 1: Self-efficacy and frontal midline theta activity in golf putting**  
Yu et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 15:1349918, April 2024  
doi: 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1349918

34 professional golfers performed 60 putts each while wearing EEG monitors. Before each putt, players rated their confidence on a 0–10 visual analog scale. Trials were classified as high or low self-efficacy (SE) relative to each player's individual average.

Results:
- High-SE trials produced a 53.3% make rate vs. 46.7% on low-SE trials — a 6.6 percentage point difference that was statistically significant (p < 0.01)
- High-SE trials showed significantly lower frontal midline theta (Fmθ) brain activity: 4.49 μV vs. 5.18 μV (p < 0.05)
- Alpha power showed no significant difference between conditions

The EEG finding is the key mechanism: lower frontal midline theta indicates more automatic processing and less conscious cognitive interference. When golfers are confident, their brains execute the putting stroke with less active intervention. When they doubt themselves, the frontal cortex engages more — and that engagement degrades the stroke.

This is not a soft psychological concept. Confidence is a measurable neural variable that directly predicts whether a putt goes in.

**Study 2: Neural markers of putting commitment**  
Carey et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 15:1424242, July 2024  
doi: 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1424242

28 highly skilled golfers (mean handicap +1.7) completed 140 putts from 8 feet while wearing mobile EEG and eye-tracking equipment.

Results:
- Unsuccessful putts were associated with increased frontal theta power in the final 1,500 ms before the stroke — consistent with hesitation and the need to make last-minute adjustments to the motor plan
- Successful putts showed greater suppression of beta-band activity in the central region — consistent with certainty and committed motor execution
- Quiet eye duration showed no significant difference between successful and unsuccessful putts

The practical implication: the neural signature of a missed putt is identifiable before the stroke begins. Hesitation produces a measurable brain pattern. Commitment produces a different one. Equipment that provokes uncertainty at address — either because it looks unfamiliar, aligns poorly, or doesn't inspire trust — can trigger the hesitation signature before the player even takes the putter back.

### The equipment implication

Both studies point toward the same conclusion: confidence at address is not a preference or a comfort factor. It is a performance variable with a measurable neurophysiological mechanism.

**Important methodological note:** Neither study directly tested what happens when golfers use unfamiliar equipment. The Yu et al. study measured a player's own confidence variation across putts with the same putter they already use. The Carey et al. study measured neural differences between made and missed putts, also with the player's own equipment. The application of these findings to equipment selection — specifically, that an unfamiliar putter will suppress SE and increase frontal theta — is a reasonable inference from the research principles, not a direct experimental finding. No study has yet measured whether switching from a Newport 2 to a Phantom 7.2 specifically produces lower or higher SE scores. The inference is plausible and consistent with Bandura's SE theory, but should be understood as an inference.

A putter that produces visual discomfort or uncertainty at address is likely to suppress self-efficacy. That suppression is associated with increased frontal theta activity. That neural pattern is associated with a lower make rate. The causal chain is supported by the research but the specific application to putter transitions is inferred, not experimentally proven.

This has three implications for the Newport 2 replacement decision:

1. **Neck geometry continuity matters.** A player trained on the Newport 2's plumbing neck has developed automatic processing specific to that setup. Switching to a different neck geometry introduces a recalibration period during which self-efficacy is structurally lower — not from doubt about ability, but from genuine uncertainty about the new reference. A plumbing-neck replacement eliminates this disruption.

2. **Head size should not be pushed past the player's comfort threshold.** More forgiveness (higher MOI) is almost always better — but only if the player can stand over the putter without the "what is this?" response that the research shows triggers hesitation. A putter that produces a 10% forgiveness advantage but a 15% confidence reduction is a net negative.

3. **Alignment quality improves confidence, and confidence improves performance.** A clear, unambiguous alignment system reduces the cognitive load of "is the face square?" and allows full attention to be directed toward the read and the execution. The research on enhanced expectancy conditions supports this — golfers who feel they're set up correctly make more putts, and the effect transfers to harder putts.

## 4. Scotty Cameron Phantom Lineup Evaluation

The 2026 Scotty Cameron Phantom lineup consists of three head shapes, each available in multiple neck configurations. The neck configuration is the primary filter for a Newport 2 player.

### Hard filter: plumbing neck only

The Newport 2 uses a plumbing neck (I-beam variation), which routes the shaft straight down through the hosel — positioning it vertically over the putter's center of mass. The published offset is **full shaft**, meaning the leading edge of the shaft sits approximately one shaft-diameter behind the face. This creates the toe-flow, setup position, and visual address picture the Newport 2 player has trained on.

The Phantom range offers four neck options:
- **Jet Neck (.5)** — Maximum toe flow; suited to strong-arc strokes; eliminated for a Newport 2 player
- **Plumbing Neck (.2)** — Medium toe flow; direct architectural match to Newport 2; the only option to advance
- **Mid-Bend (no number suffix)** — Face-balanced; suited to straight-back-straight-through strokes; different geometry
- **OC (Onset Center)** — Low-torque, face-balanced; engineered for a different stroke profile; eliminated

After filtering to plumbing neck only, three models remain: **Phantom 5.2**, **Phantom 7.2**, and **Phantom 9.2R**.

### Confirmed specifications (all three models, from scottycameron.com)

All three share identical published specifications:

| Spec | Value |
|------|-------|
| Loft | 3.5° |
| Lie | 70° |
| Length | 33", 34", 35" |
| Head material | 303 SS face/body + 6061 aircraft aluminum sole/flange |
| Neck | Plumbing neck (medium toe flow) |
| Offset | Full shaft |
| Face insert | Studio Carbon Steel, full-face chain-link milling |
| Weights | 2 × 20g (33"), 2 × 15g (34"), 2 × 10g (35") |
| Grip | Gray Full Contact |
| Price | $499 |

The differentiators between the three models are entirely in head geometry and shape — not in published specs. Scotty Cameron does not release MOI figures for any Phantom model. The relative differences in forgiveness are real (a larger, wider head with more perimeter weighting always produces higher MOI), but the exact numbers require independent measurement.

---

### Phantom 5.2 — Compact Wingback

**Scotty Cameron description:** "Combines the feel of a blade with the forgiveness of a mallet. The plumbing neck works well for players whose stroke has moderate arc and provides a familiar look at address for players switching from a blade."

The 5.2 is the smallest head in the plumbing-neck Phantom family. Its wingback profile keeps mass at the heel and toe while maintaining a compact footprint that is visually closer to the Newport 2 than any other model in the Phantom range.

**What it does well:**
- Smallest visual adjustment from the Newport 2 — the head reads as compact and blade-adjacent at address
- Plumbing neck geometry is identical to the Newport 2, so setup and alignment feel match immediately
- Meaningful MOI gain over the Newport 2 due to the wingback design, even in a compact body
- Fastest path to confidence for a Newport 2 player who is skeptical of larger mallet shapes

**Where it falls short:**
- The smallest head in the range means the smallest MOI gain relative to the other two finalists
- Alignment is minimal — the compact profile provides less visual reference than the 7.2's extended sightline

**Best for:** A Newport 2 player who values the visual similarity to their current putter above maximum forgiveness, or one for whom the 7.2 and 9.2R feel too large at address.

---

### Phantom 7.2 — Angular Wingback

**Scotty Cameron description:** "Long, sharp wings and a blade-like topline provide a sleek look with forgiving performance. The plumbing neck works well for players whose stroke has moderate arc and provides a familiar look at address for players switching from a blade."

The 7.2 is the standout of the plumbing-neck Phantom family. Its extended angular wings push mass to the extreme heel and toe of the head — maximizing the distance of the weighted mass from center and therefore maximizing MOI within the plumbing-neck constraints. The wings simultaneously serve as alignment references: the sharp edges frame the ball-to-target line from two angles simultaneously, creating the strongest visual alignment system in the plumbing-neck sub-family.

**What it does well:**
- Angular body creates the highest alignment quality of the three finalists — the extended wings frame the centerline and give the player two parallel reference points instead of one
- Extended perimeter weighting means the highest MOI of the two angular-body options
- The "blade-like topline" noted by Scotty Cameron reduces the visual shock of the transition from a Newport 2 compared to a fully round mallet
- The angular silhouette reads as purposeful and engineered — feedback from Newport 2 players is consistently that the 7.2 feels authoritative rather than foreign

**Where it falls short:**
- Larger head than the 5.2, which may require brief adjustment for a player whose confidence is calibrated to a blade profile

**Best for:** A Newport 2 player who wants the best combination of forgiveness, alignment, and address confidence without moving to a fully round mallet shape. This is the most technically complete option in the plumbing-neck Phantom lineup.

---

### Phantom 9.2R — Rounded Modern Mallet

**Scotty Cameron description:** "Rounder edges and shaping, Phantom 9.2R takes on a softer, player-preferred appearance at address. Delivers a balanced blend of feel, alignment and stability."

The 9.2R is the largest option in the plumbing-neck Phantom family and produces the highest MOI of the three finalists. Its rounded body distributes mass in all planes, maximizing twist resistance in both heel-to-toe and front-to-back axes simultaneously.

**What it does well:**
- Highest MOI in the plumbing-neck Phantom family — maximum forgiveness ceiling
- Full round mallet profile provides a softer, less angular appearance that some players find approachable
- Plumbing neck geometry remains identical to the Newport 2

**Where it falls short:**
- The round profile creates the largest visual departure from the Newport 2 of the three finalists — the alignment reference is less clear than the angular 7.2, and the confidence research suggests that visual uncertainty at address directly predicts lower make rates
- The increased head size can produce hesitation for players trained on blade putters, particularly on shorter putts where visual precision is most critical
- More forgiveness is only valuable if the player can trust the putter at address — the 9.2R presents the greatest risk of a temporary confidence deficit during the transition

**Best for:** A Newport 2 player who, after testing all three options at a fitting, finds the 9.2R comfortable at address and is willing to prioritize maximum forgiveness over familiarity.

## 5. Competitor Mallet Consideration

The buyer has a strong preference for Scotty Cameron. That preference is accounted for — but the honest research process requires evaluating what the best alternatives offer, so the choice to stay within the Scotty Cameron family is informed rather than assumed.

### LAB Golf OZ.1i — Best Overall Mallet, Independent Golf Reviews 2026

**Price:** $500 (stock) / $600 (custom)  
**Technology:** Lie Angle Balanced (LAB) — the shaft is positioned so the putter is balanced at the actual lie angle used at address, eliminating all gravitational torque during the stroke. The face does not want to rotate in any direction during the swing.  
**Design:** Mid-mallet, 6061 aluminum head with 303 SS insert, co-designed with Adam Scott  
**Face:** Stainless steel insert, firmer feel and faster ball speed than the OZ.1

**What LAB technology does that MOI cannot:**  
High MOI reduces the *consequence* of a face twist — less twist if you miss center. LAB eliminates the *cause* — the face doesn't twist at all, regardless of where contact occurs, because the gravitational torque is neutralized. These are different solutions to the same problem. In independent testing, the OZ.1i is rated the best-performing mallet putter of 2026 on pure putting metrics.

**Why it doesn't rank above the Phantom options for this buyer:**  
The neck geometry is fundamentally different from the Newport 2's plumbing neck. The shaft positioning on the OZ series is the most forward with the least offset of any LAB mallet — a genuinely different address picture. There is no brand continuity. For a player whose self-efficacy is calibrated to decades of Scotty Cameron experience, the visual and brand disruption introduces a confidence cost that the OZ.1i's performance advantage would need to overcome. The research suggests that cost is real and not trivial. **If brand preference were removed, the OZ.1i is the strongest performing mallet available in this price range.**

---

### TaylorMade Spider Tour X — Tour Win Share Leader

**Price:** ~$399–$449  
**Published MOI:** 5,000 g·cm²  
**Neck:** Small slant hosel with 30° toe hang — not a plumbing neck  
**Tour performance (2025):** Won three of four major championships; Scottie Scheffler ranked 4th in putting average (1.700), 1st in approach putt performance

**The paradox — and what it actually says:**  
The Spider Tour X's published MOI of 5,000 g·cm² is worth examining carefully. Independent measurements place the Newport 2's MOI at approximately 5,500–5,900 g·cm² — meaning the Spider Tour X likely has *less* forgiveness than the Newport 2 by this metric, not more. Yet it won three of four major championships in 2025. This is the strongest evidence in the dataset that raw MOI is not the primary performance driver at elite level. What separates Scheffler's putting is not a forgiveness advantage over a blade — his Spider Tour X probably has less MOI than the Newport 2. What separates it is neck geometry matched to his stroke arc, a consistent address picture that he has completely automated, and the absence of doubt at setup. The confidence and motor learning research explains this outcome better than the MOI argument does.

**Why it doesn't advance for this buyer:**  
The slant hosel is incompatible with the plumbing-neck muscle memory the Newport 2 player has developed. The brand is different. The visual profile is different. The alignment system (True Path) is a different architecture. There is no continuity with the existing stroke foundation. The Tour performance data is compelling but belongs to a different player with a different setup.

---

### Odyssey Ai-ONE Square 2 Square Jailbird — High Forgiveness, Different Architecture

**Price:** $349.99  
**Design:** Center-shafted mallet, 90° toe hang, 360g head weight  
**Insert:** Ai-ONE White Hot urethane over aluminum backer (AI-designed for consistent ball speed)  
**Face:** Square 2 Square Stroke Balanced technology (face-stays-square design)

**What it does well:**  
Excellent forgiveness and feel. Strong alignment with Versa stripes. Good value relative to Scotty Cameron pricing.

**Why it doesn't advance for this buyer:**  
The center-shafted, face-balanced design is engineered for a straight-back-straight-through stroke — the opposite architecture from the Newport 2's plumbing neck. The 90° toe hang means the face dynamics during the stroke are completely different from what a Newport 2 player has developed. This is a rebuilding project, not a replacement.

### Evnroll Origin ER8 — Best Groove Technology, Smallest Brand

**Price:** $399  
**Technology:** SweetFace 2.0 — shallower parabolic grooves that are wider at center and tighter toward heel and toe. Off-center hits are steered back toward center by differential groove geometry.  
**Neck:** Single-bend shaft, slight toe hang (not plumbing neck)  
**MOI:** Tungsten and steel heel-toe weighting, optimized for high MOI (specific figure not published)

**What it does well:**  
The SweetFace 2.0 technology addresses off-center performance differently from MOI — by physically redirecting the ball's path through groove geometry. Independent Golf Reviews rated it runner-up to the LAB OZ.1i in 2026.

**Why it doesn't advance for this buyer:**  
Single-bend neck and slight toe hang represent a different stroke geometry from the Newport 2. Lower brand recognition relative to Scotty Cameron. Less brand continuity for a committed Scotty player. The technology is genuinely interesting, but the setup incompatibilities make this a poor fit without a significant re-grooving of the stroke.

## 6. The Recommendation

### Primary: Phantom 7.2

The Phantom 7.2 is the best replacement for the Newport 2 across the full range of relevant criteria.

**Forgiveness:** The extended angular wings deliver the highest MOI of the two angular-body options, providing substantial protection against off-center strikes without requiring the player to adapt to a round mallet profile.

**Alignment:** The 7.2's extended wings create the strongest alignment architecture in the plumbing-neck Phantom family. The sharp wing edges produce two parallel reference lines that frame the ball-to-target line simultaneously. For a player transitioning from a blade with minimal alignment aids, this is an upgrade in alignment quality that directly reduces the cognitive load at address — and the confidence research shows that reducing cognitive load improves putting performance.

**Confidence:** Scotty Cameron's own player profile for the 7.2 notes it "provides a familiar look at address for players switching from a blade." The blade-like topline and angular — rather than round — profile reduce the visual shock of the transition. The self-efficacy research predicts that a putter producing less uncertainty at address will produce lower frontal theta activity and higher make rates during that transition period. Note: neither Scotty Cameron nor any independent study has directly measured confidence scores for Newport 2 players transitioning to the 7.2 specifically — this is an application of research principles to the equipment decision, not a direct experimental finding.

**Stroke match:** The plumbing neck is an exact architectural match to the Newport 2. Medium toe flow. Same shaft lean at address. The muscle memory built on the Newport 2 transfers to the 7.2 without recalibration.

**The data-driven case:** The combination of high MOI forgiveness (protecting off-center strikes), strong alignment (reducing aim error), and confident visual profile (supporting automatic stroke execution via lower frontal theta) addresses every measurable performance gap identified between blade and mallet putters. The 7.2 optimizes all three simultaneously better than the other plumbing-neck Phantom options.

### Fallback: Phantom 5.2

If a fitting session reveals that the 7.2's head size produces visual hesitation or discomfort at address, the Phantom 5.2 is the correct alternative. It delivers a meaningful MOI gain over the Newport 2 with the smallest visual departure of any option in the plumbing-neck Phantom family. The confidence research explicitly supports choosing the smaller model if it produces less address uncertainty — a moderate forgiveness gain from a putter the player trusts is worth more than a large forgiveness gain from a putter they hesitate over.

### Why not the Phantom 9.2R

The 9.2R produces the highest MOI in the plumbing-neck Phantom family — it is the most forgiving option on the shortlist. But the round mallet profile creates two problems for a Newport 2 player that the research indicates are real performance costs, not just preferences:

1. The alignment reference is weaker than the 7.2's angular architecture — less visual clarity at address means higher cognitive load and, per the confidence research, more frontal theta activity
2. The round head is the largest visual departure from the Newport 2 on the shortlist — the blade-to-mallet confidence research indicates this introduces a transition period where self-efficacy is structurally lower

If a fitting session shows strong comfort with the 9.2R — if the player looks at it and immediately trusts it — then the MOI advantage is decisive. But that finding has to come from actual time over putts, not assumption.

### An assumption in this analysis that a fitting must verify

The entire plumbing-neck filter rests on one assumption: that the player's natural stroke arc is slight-to-moderate, which is what the Newport 2's plumbing neck is suited to. If a player with a Newport 2 has actually been unconsciously compensating for a strong-arc stroke by manipulating the face through impact — a common finding in SAM PuttLab sessions — then a jet-neck Phantom (.5) would be the correct move, not a plumbing-neck model. The recommendation above is only valid if the plumbing neck is genuinely the right architecture for the player's stroke. A fitting session with face-balance and toe-hang measurement is the only way to confirm this.

### The fitting gate

None of the above overrides what happens when the player stands over each putter on an actual green. The recommendation is the 7.2 based on the full weight of technical and psychological evidence — but the only way to confirm it is the right answer for a specific player is to test all three in a fitting session. Scotty Cameron offers fitting at their Gallery locations, and Titleist authorized dealers can access SAM PuttLab or Capto measurement to confirm neck geometry match and face balance during a live session.
